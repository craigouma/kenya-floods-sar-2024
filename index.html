Key finding

A U-Net trained only on Sen1Floods11, which contains no Kenyan data, agrees with UNOSAT's flood maps along the Tana about twice as well as the best threshold or change-detection rule (IoU 0.347 against 0.160), and its flooded area matches UNOSAT's to within about 1% on two references. The same model finds only 3 to 14% of the buildings UNOSAT counted as affected, because its masks stay out of settlements, while a simple change-detection rule comes within the range expected from building data alone. In Nairobi, methods that keep their false alarms in check find about a quarter of the mapped flooding.

Abstract

Kenya's March to May 2024 long rains brought flooding along the Tana River and across Nairobi and its neighbouring counties, and UNOSAT mapped it from optical satellite imagery, which cloud blocks during the rains. This paper measures how well Sentinel-1 radar flood mapping methods developed elsewhere transfer to these Kenyan landscapes, using UNOSAT's maps as an independent reference. Nine methods were compared, from Otsu thresholds and change detection to U-Nets trained on Sen1Floods11, with no Kenyan data used in training. In riverine settings, at Garissa and along 8,785 km² of the Tana and Galana rivers, the main U-Net agreed with UNOSAT at an IoU of 0.347 (95% interval 0.331 to 0.364), against 0.160 for the best classical method, and its flooded area was within about 1% of UNOSAT's on two references. In the urban analysis of Nairobi, Kiambu and Machakos every method's interval overlapped, and built-up land was where all nine failed. Timing mattered as much as method: at Garissa the U-Net scored 0.215 against a reference three days after the radar pass and 0.581 against one nine days before it, because the river was still rising. The best extent model placed only 3 to 14% of UNOSAT's affected buildings inside its masks, so extent and exposure call for different products. Applied to every pass from April to June, the U-Net shows flood water on the middle Tana peaking at 535 km² on 20 May and near the delta about four weeks later.

Scope note

The reference is UNOSAT's rapid mapping of activation FL20240426KEN, drawn from Sentinel-2 and Pléiades imagery and described by UNOSAT as preliminary and not field-validated. It is treated as an independent reference, not ground truth. No Kenyan or UNOSAT data were used to train any model. Full reasoning for every choice is in the repository's DECISIONS.md. This is independent research and is not produced, reviewed, commissioned, or endorsed by UNOSAT, UNITAR, ESA, the Copernicus programme, NDMA, the Kenya Space Agency, or the Kenya Red Cross.

What the evaluation shows
1

A public model transfers to Kenyan floodplains

The Sen1Floods11 U-Net scores 0.424 all-water IoU on the benchmark's own test chips, in line with the published 0.408, and agrees with UNOSAT at Garissa on 5 May at an IoU of 0.544.

2

Built-up land defeats every method

F1 for the flood class on built-up land is below 0.11 for all nine methods in both settings. Cropland and herbaceous wetland are where the methods work best.

3

The day gap matters more than the method

At Garissa the U-Net's map from a radar pass three days before the 29 April optical image agrees at an IoU of 0.22, and from a pass nine days after it at 0.58: the Tana was still rising. In Nairobi, passes five days either side of the reference find almost nothing.

4

Extent and exposure need different products

The method that maps flood extent best misses most flooded buildings, and the one that counts buildings best maps extent poorly.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The March to May 2024 long rains were near or above average over most of
Kenya, and at several Nairobi stations, at Thika and at Machakos they were the
wettest season on record \cite{kmd2024}. Flooding followed along the Tana River
in Garissa and Tana River counties and across Nairobi and the neighbouring
counties of Kiambu and Machakos, and landslides struck Mai Mahiu and Kijabe
in Nakuru and Kiambu \cite{unosat2024}. By 18 June the National Disaster Operations Centre counted
315 people killed, 188 injured and 38 missing, and more than 293,200 people
displaced since 1 March \cite{ocha2024fu7}.

The United Nations Satellite Centre (UNOSAT) activated rapid mapping for the
event under code FL20240426KEN and published flood extents for Garissa and
the village of Mororo opposite it, for the Tana and Galana rivers, and for the
built-up area spanning Nairobi, Kiambu and Machakos \cite{unosat2024}. Those
maps were drawn from optical imagery, from Sentinel-2 and from very high
resolution Pléiades scenes, because optical imagery is what a human analyst
can interpret quickly. Optical sensors cannot see through cloud, and the long
rains are, by definition, the cloudiest part of the year. In the six days
either side of UNOSAT's reference dates, the three Sentinel-2 acquisitions over
Nairobi were 52\,\%, 89\,\% and 92\,\% cloud covered and the three over
Garissa 80\,\%, 36\,\% and 100\,\%, as reported for the whole 110\,km tile.

Sentinel-1 carries a C-band synthetic aperture radar (SAR) that images through
cloud, by day and night, and its data are free. Since the loss of Sentinel-1B in
December 2021, coverage has thinned: Garissa, for example, was imaged on a
single track every 12 days through 2024. Open water is dark in SAR because it
reflects the radar pulse away from the sensor, and this contrast is the basis
of every SAR flood mapping method.

The methods available off the shelf were developed or trained elsewhere.
Thresholding methods were tuned on European and South Asian floods, and the
most widely used labelled dataset for training deep learning models on
Sentinel-1, Sen1Floods11 \cite{bonafilia2020}, spans eleven flood events in
eleven countries, three of them African. Whether these methods hold up on
Kenyan landscapes, where semi-arid floodplains, cropland, informal
settlements and dense urban fabric each scatter radar differently, has not
been measured against an independent reference. This paper measures it.

The contributions are four. First, classical and deep learning Sentinel-1
methods are run over the 2024 event and scored against UNOSAT's reference
maps, separately for riverine and urban settings and broken down by land
cover, with uncertainty from a spatial block bootstrap. Second, the
sensitivity of that agreement to the number of days between the radar and
optical acquisitions is measured directly, by scoring every Sentinel-1 pass
within 24 days of each reference. Third, building exposure estimated from each
method is compared with UNOSAT's counts of affected structures. Fourth, the
method that performs best in riverine settings is applied to every pass over
the lower Tana between April and June 2024, as an application with its
measured error attached.

The paper is the companion to an earlier study in this series on satellite
indicators of drought and food insecurity in northern Kenya \cite{ouma2026drought},
and covers the opposite hydrological hazard with a second sensor type.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{SAR flood mapping}

The simplest method classifies a single post-event image by a backscatter
threshold. Because the threshold depends on sensor, incidence angle, wind and
land cover, it is usually fitted per scene, most often with Otsu's method
\cite{otsu1979}, which chooses the threshold that maximises the separation of
a bimodal histogram. A histogram over a whole scene is rarely bimodal when
water covers a small fraction of it, so split-based approaches fit the
threshold only on tiles that contain both classes
\cite{martinis2009,chini2017}; the same idea underlies fully automated
processing chains for Sentinel-1 \cite{twele2016,martinis2015}.

Change detection compares the flood image with a reference image from dry
conditions, usually as a log ratio, and labels as flooded the pixels that
became darker \cite{bazi2005,hostache2012}. It removes permanently dark
surfaces such as tarmac, sand and smooth bare soil, which single-image
thresholds confuse with water, at the cost of needing a suitable reference
image from the same orbit \cite{hostache2012}. Terrain descriptors such as
Height Above the Nearest Drainage (HAND) \cite{renno2008,nobre2011} are used
to remove water-like pixels on slopes and hilltops where flooding is
implausible.

The Copernicus Emergency Management Service now runs a global flood
monitoring product on every Sentinel-1 acquisition, as an ensemble of three
independently developed algorithms \cite{salamon2021,krullikowski2023}.

\subsection{Sen1Floods11}

Sen1Floods11 \cite{bonafilia2020} provides 4,831 chips of 512 by 512 pixels at
10\,m from eleven flood events, each with Sentinel-1 VV and VH backscatter
processed in Google Earth Engine. 446 chips carry hand-checked labels and are
split into training, validation and test sets, with the Bolivia event held out
as an unseen test; the remaining 4,385 carry weak labels from automated
classifications of Sentinel-1 or of coincident Sentinel-2. In the original
paper the best flood-water results came from a fully convolutional network
trained on the Sentinel-2-derived weak labels, with a mean per-chip IoU of
0.339 for flood water and 0.408 for all water on the test set, against 0.285
and 0.359 for Otsu thresholding of VH. Later work has reported Sentinel-1-only
results on the same benchmark with other architectures and loss functions
\cite{bai2021,konapala2021}; the highest published Sen1Floods11 numbers come
from optical foundation models \cite{jakubik2023,szwarcman2024}, which do not
use the radar channels.

\subsection{The urban SAR problem}

In built-up areas, walls and the ground form corner reflectors, and floodwater
between buildings strengthens this double-bounce return instead of darkening
it \cite{mason2012,mason2014}. Radar shadow and layover behind tall structures
add dark and bright artefacts unrelated to water. Floodwater in towns is
therefore often invisible to backscatter thresholds, and approaches that work
there rely on interferometric coherence or on combining SAR with an elevation
model of the urban surface \cite{mason2021}. The Sen1Floods11 validation data
include no urban flood events \cite{bonafilia2020}.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Reference maps and their limits}

UNOSAT published twelve products for activation FL20240426KEN on the
Humanitarian Data Exchange under CC BY-SA \cite{unosat2024}. Every product
page links the same cumulative shapefile package, and the products resolve
into fewer independent analyses than pages (Table~\ref{tab:aois}). The four
urban flood products are views of one Pléiades analysis of 1 May 2024 with a
single 580\,km$^2$ extent, flood layer and structure layer; their reported
structure counts (3,387, 3,663, 4,246 and 1,815) sum to exactly the 13,111
points in that layer. They are treated here as one area of interest (AOI). The
Sentinel-2 analysis of Garissa on 29 April has no flood layer of its own in the
package, and its flood water is taken from the Tana and Galana product derived
from the same image, clipped to the Garissa extent; this gives 27.9\,km$^2$
against the 26\,km$^2$ UNOSAT reports. The Tana and Galana product covers
70,874\,km$^2$ and has no product page of its own. The national NOAA-VIIRS
composite (375\,m pixels), the Mai Mahiu landslide product and three cropland
subsets of the urban analysis are not used.

These maps are an independent reference, not ground truth. Each product
states that it is a preliminary analysis not yet validated in the field. They
were digitised from optical images by analysts working to a deadline, at
resolutions from 0.5\,m (Pléiades) to 10\,m (Sentinel-2), and they record
water as seen from above: flooding under tree canopy, or water so shallow or
turbid that it is spectrally indistinguishable from wet soil, may be missed or
mapped inconsistently. They were also acquired on different days from the
radar. Disagreement between a SAR method and these maps therefore combines
errors of the method, errors of the reference and real change between the
two acquisitions. Every UNOSAT flood layer used here is classed as new flood
water, separate from the river, and UNOSAT's cloud-obstruction polygons,
where its analysts could not see the ground, are removed from the evaluation
domain.

\subsection{Sentinel-1 imagery}

Sentinel-1 Interferometric Wide swath Ground Range Detected (GRD) scenes in VV
and VH polarisation were read from Microsoft Planetary Computer
\cite{planetary2022}. Its radiometrically terrain corrected collection now
requires an account, so the GRD scenes were processed here: thermal noise
removal, calibration to $\sigma^0$ from the product's annotation tables, and
geocoding from the annotation geolocation grid onto a 10\,m UTM zone 37S grid,
with each pixel shifted in range by $(h - h_\mathrm{ann})/\tan\theta$ for its
height $h$ above the ellipsoid, where $h_\mathrm{ann}$ is the terrain height
the GRD was projected to and $\theta$ the incidence angle. Heights come from
the Copernicus DEM GLO-30 \cite{copdem} converted from EGM2008 to ellipsoidal
height (the geoid lies 27\,m below the ellipsoid at Garissa and 16\,m at
Nairobi). No radiometric terrain flattening is applied. This reproduces the
processing behind the Sen1Floods11 chips. On 18 hand-labelled chips from nine
events, processing the original scenes this way reproduced the Sen1Floods11
values with a median bias between $-0.02$ and $+0.04$\,dB and a median
correlation of 0.97, with zero or one pixel of offset for all events except
one, so models trained on Sen1Floods11 see Kenyan inputs from the same
radiometric domain.

For each AOI the post-event pass is the one closest in time to the reference
image, and the pre-event pass is the latest January or February 2024
acquisition from the same relative orbit. A pass is all slices of one
datatake that image the AOI, mosaicked. Garissa is covered by a single track
(relative orbit 159, ascending) and the Nairobi area by one other (relative
orbit 57, ascending).

\subsection{Training data}

Sen1Floods11 v1.1 \cite{bonafilia2020} was downloaded from its public bucket.
Three of its eleven events are African: Ghana (53 hand-labelled chips),
Nigeria (18) and Somalia (26). Somalia, the Juba and Shabelle floods of May
2018, is the only East African event, so the transfer to Kenya is out of
region for most of the training data but not all of it. No Kenyan data, and
no UNOSAT data, were used in training.

\subsection{Ancillary layers}

Permanent water is taken from JRC Global Surface Water occurrence
\cite{pekel2016}, land cover from ESA WorldCover 2021 at 10\,m
\cite{zanaga2022}, HAND from the global GLO-30 HAND layer derived from the
Copernicus DEM, buildings from Google Open Buildings v3 \cite{sirko2021} and
county boundaries from the Kenya common operational dataset on the
Humanitarian Data Exchange, as in the companion study
\cite{ouma2026drought}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Classical methods}

Four classical methods were run, each on VV and on VH. \textbf{Otsu
thresholding} labels as water every pixel of the 3\,$\times$\,3 focal mean of
post-event backscatter below a threshold fitted with Otsu's method
\cite{otsu1979}. Following the Sen1Floods11 reference classification
\cite{bonafilia2020}, the threshold is fitted on the pooled histogram of the
most heterogeneous 1\,km tiles (the top 10\,\%, at least five), since a
histogram dominated by dry land is not bimodal. \textbf{Change detection}
uses the difference in dB between the smoothed post-event and pre-event images,
which is the log ratio of the two, and labels as flooded the pixels that
darkened by more than a threshold. Two thresholds are compared: Otsu's,
fitted the same way and capped at 0\,dB so that brightening is never labelled
as flooding, and a fixed $-3$\,dB.

\subsection{U-Nets trained on Sen1Floods11}

A four-level U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015} with 32 base channels and group
normalisation was trained on Sentinel-1 VV and VH chips. Training follows the
original paper's setting that gave its best flood-water results: the
hand-labelled training split plus the weakly labelled chips with labels
derived from Sentinel-2, random 256\,$\times$\,256 crops with flips, cross
entropy weighted 1:8 towards water, AdamW with learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$
and weight decay $10^{-2}$, and cosine annealing with warm restarts. Inputs
are scaled exactly as in the reference code. The Bolivia event is held out
entirely, including the weakly labelled Bolivia chips that the public bucket
contains, and the checkpoint with the best mean per-chip IoU on the
validation split is kept. Training ran for 150 epochs on a single Kaggle T4
GPU.

Sen1Floods11 has no pre-event imagery. To test whether a model that sees the
dry-season image does better, pre-event chips were built for the
hand-labelled set with the processing described above, from the latest pass
on the same relative orbit at least 30 days before each event; 419 of the 446
chips have one. A U-Net with four input channels (post and pre VV and VH) was
trained on the hand-labelled chips that have a pair, alongside a control with
the post-event channels only, trained on the same chips. The two differ only in
their inputs.

\subsection{Post-processing}

Every method's output is post-processed identically. Pixels with JRC water
occurrence above 80\,\% are removed from the prediction and from the
evaluation domain. A second variant also sets to dry every pixel more than
20\,m above the nearest drainage. The JRC rule removes almost nothing along
the Tana at Garissa, where occurrence peaks at 72\,\% because the channel
migrates and carries sediment.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Agreement is measured for the flood class as intersection over union (IoU),
F1, precision and recall over the evaluation domain: the UNOSAT analysis
extent minus cloud obstruction, permanent water, and pixels without radar
data. AOIs are grouped as riverine and rural (Garissa and the Tana and Galana
corridor) and urban and peri-urban (the Nairobi, Kiambu and Machakos
analysis), and the groups are reported separately throughout because SAR
behaves differently in built-up areas. Within a group, confusion counts are
pooled across AOIs. The 95\,\% intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap resamples
of 1\,km blocks rather than pixels, since neighbouring pixels are strongly
correlated. Results are also broken down by ESA WorldCover class.

The Tana and Galana product cannot be evaluated over its whole 70,874\,km$^2$
without scoring mostly dry land far from any river. It is evaluated along a
corridor defined independently of the reference: land within 5\,km of any
300\,m cell where JRC water occurrence reaches 50\,\%, cut into 20\,km tiles
and excluding the Garissa extent, which is scored on its own. The corridor
covers 8,785\,km$^2$ and contains 361 of the 469\,km$^2$ of flood water in
that product.

To measure the effect of the time between the radar and optical images, every
Sentinel-1 pass within 24 days of each point AOI's reference date was run
through the same methods and scored against the same reference.

Building exposure is the number of Open Buildings v3 centroids with confidence
of at least 0.70 inside each flood mask and the evaluation domain. UNOSAT
counted affected structures on its own imagery, not on Open Buildings, so the
number of Open Buildings centroids inside UNOSAT's own flood extent is reported
as well, to separate disagreement in the building data from disagreement in
the flood extent.

\section{Results: Sen1Floods11 test performance}
\label{sec:s1f}

Table~\ref{tab:s1f} scores every method on the Sen1Floods11 test split and on
the held-out Bolivia event, with the original paper's convention (the mean of
per-chip IoUs) and with IoU pooled over all pixels. The U-Net trained on
hand-labelled and Sentinel-2-derived weak labels reaches a mean per-chip IoU
of 0.424 for all water and 0.358 for flood water on the test split, and 0.446
and 0.392 on Bolivia. The original paper's best model, trained on the same
labels, reported 0.408 and 0.339 on the test split and 0.316 and 0.274 on
Bolivia \cite{bonafilia2020}. The model is therefore in line with, and
slightly above, the published range, and its Kenyan results can be read as
those of a competent Sen1Floods11 model. Its best validation score came at
epoch 121 of 150.

Otsu thresholding of VH, fitted on heterogeneous tiles of each chip, scores
0.224 for flood water on the test split and 0.387 on Bolivia, against 0.285
and 0.324 reported for the original per-event threshold. Change detection,
which can only be scored on the 86 test and 9 Bolivia chips that have a
pre-event image, scores between 0.10 and 0.12 on the test split. The
pre-event images were drawn from one to five months before events that mostly
occurred within long wet seasons, and many are probably not dry; the same
method has a dry-season reference in Kenya.

On the paired chips, giving the U-Net the pre-event image changes flood-water
IoU from 0.332 to 0.337 on the test split, within noise, and from 0.322 to
0.410 on Bolivia, the one event no model saw in training.

\section{Results: transfer to Kenya}
\label{sec:kenya}

\subsection{Riverine and urban agreement}

Table~\ref{tab:groups} gives agreement with UNOSAT pooled within each group,
and Figure~\ref{fig:iou} plots it. In riverine and rural settings the U-Net
trained on hand-labelled and weak labels agrees best with the reference, with
an IoU of 0.347 (95\,\% interval 0.331 to 0.364), roughly double the best
classical method, change detection on VV at a fixed $-3$\,dB (0.160, 0.150
to 0.172). The intervals do not overlap. Single-image Otsu thresholds and
Otsu-fitted change detection do worst, with precision between 0.07 and 0.15:
they label as water far more than UNOSAT mapped.

In the urban and peri-urban analysis the ordering compresses. The three U-Nets
score between 0.217 and 0.228 and the best classical method, Otsu on VH,
0.192 (0.161 to 0.225); the intervals overlap, and no method can be said to be
better than the others. Every method's precision there is moderate (0.62 to
0.65 for the U-Nets) and its recall low (about 0.26): what SAR labels as water
in Nairobi is usually water in the reference, but it finds about a quarter of
the flood water UNOSAT mapped.

\subsection{Individual areas}

The pooled riverine figure is dominated by the Tana and Galana corridor, which
holds 360 of the 410\,km$^2$ of riverine reference flood water. Within it the
U-Net's IoU is 0.344 and change detection's 0.147. Change detection's errors
there are mostly commission in shrubland, grassland and tree cover away from
the channels: patches that darkened between February and late April but that
UNOSAT did not map as flood water. They may be rain-filled pans and
depressions on the semi-arid plain, flooding under vegetation that an optical
image cannot see, or false alarms, and the data here cannot separate the
three. The U-Net's flooded area over the corridor, 356\,km$^2$, is within
1\,\% of the reference's 360\,km$^2$.

At Garissa the two references give very different pictures. Against the
Pléiades map of 5 May, matched by a radar pass three days later, the U-Net
reaches an IoU of 0.544 with precision and recall both near 0.70, and its
flooded area, 21.5\,km$^2$, is within 1\,\% of the reference's 21.7\,km$^2$.
Change detection on VV reaches 0.487. Against the Sentinel-2 map of 29 April,
matched by a pass three days earlier, every method falls to between 0.10 and
0.22, with recall below 0.25. Figure~\ref{fig:panels} shows the 5 May case.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the day gap}

The Garissa contrast is a timing effect, which scoring every Sentinel-1 pass
within 24 days of each reference shows directly (Figure~\ref{fig:daygap}).
Against the 29 April reference, change detection on VV scores 0.163 with the
26 April pass and 0.443 with the 8 May pass, nine days after the reference.
Against the 5 May reference it scores 0.487 with the pass three days later and
0.174 with the pass nine days earlier. The radar mapped 7.3\,km$^2$ of flood
water on 26 April and 16.7 to 18.1\,km$^2$ on 8 May: the Tana was still
rising at Garissa in late April, and the 29 April optical image caught a
larger flood than the radar three days before it. Passes 15 or more days from
either reference score below 0.07. In Nairobi, agreement exists only on the
day of the reference, 0.151 for change detection, against 0.009 to 0.025 for
passes 12 to 24 days away, as expected for short-lived urban flooding.

\subsection{Land cover}

Figure~\ref{fig:landcover} breaks agreement down by ESA WorldCover class.
Built-up land is the class where every method fails, in both groups: F1 for
the flood class is below 0.11 for all nine methods. UNOSAT itself mapped
little flood water on land WorldCover classes as built-up (0.7\,km$^2$ in the
riverine AOIs and 2.7\,km$^2$ in the urban one, against 30.5\,km$^2$ on
grassland in the urban analysis, mostly along river corridors), so these
scores rest on small areas, but the failure is uniform. The best class is
cropland for the U-Nets (F1 0.68 riverine, 0.54 urban) and herbaceous wetland
for the classical methods in riverine settings (0.53 to 0.60). For the U-Net,
riverine shrubland is the weakest vegetated class (F1 0.21).

\subsection{HAND mask}

Setting to dry every pixel more than 20\,m above the nearest drainage changes
